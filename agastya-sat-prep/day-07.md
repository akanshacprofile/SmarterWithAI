## MATH LESSON (15 min)

### Topic: Rational Expressions

A rational expression is a fraction with polynomials. The key skill is **simplifying** and **solving equations** with them.

#### Simplifying — factor and cancel
`(x² − 9) / (x² − x − 6)` → factor both:
- Top: (x − 3)(x + 3)
- Bottom: (x − 3)(x + 2)
- Cancel (x − 3): **(x + 3) / (x + 2)**

#### Adding/subtracting — common denominator
`1/x + 1/(x+1)` = `(x+1)/[x(x+1)] + x/[x(x+1)]` = **(2x + 1) / [x(x+1)]**

#### Solving rational equations
Multiply both sides by the LCD to clear fractions.

#### Worked example 1
Solve `3/x + 1 = 5/x`
- Multiply by x: `3 + x = 5`
- **x = 2**

#### Worked example 2
Solve `1/(x − 1) = 2/(x + 3)`
- Cross-multiply: `(x + 3) = 2(x − 1)`
- x + 3 = 2x − 2 → **x = 5**

#### Excluded values
A rational expression is undefined where the denominator = 0. Always state x ≠ those values.
- `1/(x − 4)` → undefined at x = 4.

#### Speed tip
For rational equations: cross-multiply only when there's ONE fraction on each side. Otherwise use LCD.

## ENGLISH LESSON (15 min)

### Topic: Modifier Placement (Dangling & Misplaced Modifiers)

**Rule:** A modifier (especially an opening phrase) must clearly describe the noun it's nearest to — usually the subject right after the comma.

#### Dangling modifier
The modifier describes nothing in the sentence, or the wrong thing.
- ❌ "Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful."
  → Trees aren't walking. The sentence has no walker.
- ✓ "Walking down the street, **I** noticed the beautiful trees."

#### Misplaced modifier
- ❌ "She nearly drove her kids to school every day for ten years."
  → She nearly drove them? (Did she actually drive?)
- ✓ "She drove her kids to school **nearly** every day for ten years."

#### SAT pattern
When you see an opening phrase ending in a comma, the next noun MUST be what the phrase describes.

#### Worked example 1
"Crossing the finish line, ____ erupted in cheers."
Options: (A) the crowd (B) cheers were heard (C) people heard cheers
- The runner is crossing the line. Best fit: **(A) the crowd erupted** — wait, but is the crowd crossing the line? No. None of those make logical sense for "crossing the finish line."
- This is the trap. The correct fix usually inserts the actual runner: "Crossing the finish line, **the runner** heard the crowd erupt in cheers."

#### Speed tip
Opening modifier + comma → next word must be the noun the modifier describes. If it's not, the answer is wrong.
